What Is PGP?

PGP (Pretty Good Privacy) is a system of public-key cryptography. Instead of sharing one secret password, you create a key pair:

  • Public key — safe to share with anyone. It is used to encrypt messages to you.
  • Private key — kept secret. It is the only thing that can decrypt messages sent to you.

The two keys are mathematically linked, but you cannot figure out the private key from the public key. This is what makes the system work: you can hand your public key to a stranger, and they can encrypt a message that only you can read — even if the message is intercepted.

What Does PGP Do?

PGP gives you three superpowers:

  • Confidentiality — only the intended recipient can read the message.
  • Authentication — you can prove a message really came from you (using signatures).
  • Integrity — you can detect if a message was tampered with in transit.

In short, PGP turns readable text into scrambled ciphertext that only the right private key can unscramble. Think of it as the difference between sending a postcard (anyone can read it) and sending a locked safe (only the person with the key can open it).

The Basic Flow

Once you have a key pair, using PGP is a four-step process:

  1. Generate a key pair on the Encrypt / Decrypt page.
  2. Share your public key with friends or paste it into My Profile.
  3. Encrypt a message using the recipient's public key.
  4. Send the encrypted block however you like — email, chat, notes.

When someone sends you an encrypted message, you use your private key to decrypt it. The sender used your public key to encrypt, and only your private key can reverse it.

When Should You Use PGP?

Use PGP whenever you want extra protection for information:

  • Sending sensitive personal information over email or chat.
  • Sharing passwords, account recovery codes, or financial details.
  • Signing files or messages to prove they came from you.
  • Protecting notes, backups, or files stored in the cloud.

You do not need to encrypt everything — PGP is for the moments when privacy and trust matter most. Encrypting your lunch plans is overkill. Encrypting your tax documents is wise.

Key Insight: PGP protects the contents of your messages, not the metadata. An attacker watching your traffic still knows you sent a message, to whom, and when — they just cannot read what it says. For hiding the fact that you communicated at all, you need anonymity tools like Tor or Signal.

What Is Threat Modeling?

Threat modeling is the foundation of all OPSEC (operational security). Before you choose any tool — PGP, Signal, a password manager — you need to answer three questions:

  1. What am I protecting? — Messages, files, passwords, your identity?
  2. Who am I protecting it from? — A nosy roommate, an employer, a hacker, a government?
  3. What happens if I fail? — Embarrassment, financial loss, legal trouble, physical danger?

Your answers determine which tools you need and how much effort to invest. There is no one-size-fits-all answer. A journalist protecting a whistleblower's identity needs far stronger protections than someone hiding a surprise party plan.

The Adversary Spectrum

Think about who might want your data, and what resources they have:

  • Low-resource: A curious friend or coworker looking over your shoulder.
  • Medium-resource: A hacker trying to steal credentials for profit.
  • High-resource: A well-funded attacker, employer, or state actor with time and tools.

PGP protects the contents of your messages. It does not hide who you are talking to (metadata). If an adversary knowing you communicated at all is dangerous, PGP alone is not enough — you also need to think about traffic analysis, anonymity networks, and more.

Key Insight: Perfect security does not exist. The goal is to raise the cost of an attack higher than what your adversary is willing to pay. A 12-character random password stops a casual hacker; it will not stop a state intelligence agency with a billion-dollar budget.

A Simple Threat Model Exercise

Try this for your own situation. Grab a piece of paper and write down:

  • Your most sensitive piece of digital information.
  • Who would want it and why.
  • The worst realistic consequence if it were exposed.
  • One thing you could do today to make that exposure less likely.

That last point is your security action item. Do it. Then come back and do the exercise again for the next asset. Threat modeling is not a one-time activity — it is a habit.

DO: Match your tools to your threat model. If you only need to keep a roommate out, a password is fine. If you are a source talking to a journalist, use PGP, verify fingerprints in person, and consider anonymity tools.
DON'T: Assume more encryption is always better. Encrypting everything, including trivial messages, can actually attract attention from high-resource adversaries. Be deliberate about what you protect.

Creating Keys

A PGP key pair consists of a public key (share freely) and a private key (guard with your life). When you generate a key, a few choices matter:

  • Key size: RSA-2048 is the practical minimum. RSA-4096 is stronger and recommended for anything sensitive. Larger keys are slower but harder to crack.
  • Algorithm: RSA is the most widely supported. EdDSA and ECDH are newer and faster, but RSA remains the default in most beginner tools.
  • Passphrase: Your private key should be encrypted with a strong passphrase so that stealing the key file is not enough to use it.

Storing Keys

Where you keep your private key is one of the most important security decisions you will make:

DO: Keep your private key on your own device, encrypted with a strong passphrase. Store a backup offline (USB drive in a safe) so you do not lose it.
DON'T: Email your private key to yourself, store it in a cloud note, or leave it on a shared computer. This app stores keys server-side only for practice — real PGP tools never do this.
Practice vs. Reality: This learning tool stores your private key on the server so beginners cannot lose it. In a real PGP setup, your private key should live only on your own device. Never put a real private key on someone else's server.

Rotating Keys

Keys do not last forever. You should generate a new key pair and retire the old one when:

  • You suspect or know your private key was compromised.
  • Your passphrase was leaked or guessed.
  • The key is several years old and you want stronger cryptography.
  • You are changing your primary identity or email address.

When you rotate, publish the new public key and sign it with your old key (if you still trust it) so your contacts know the new key really is yours.

Retiring Keys

When you stop using a key, revoke it. A revocation certificate tells everyone who has your public key that it should no longer be trusted. Generate the revocation certificate when you create the key and store it somewhere safe — you may not have access to the key later when you need to revoke it.

Old keys that are not revoked can be a liability: someone who finds a compromised private key can still decrypt old messages that were encrypted to it, or impersonate you to anyone who still has the old public key.

The Key Hygiene Checklist

  1. Generate keys with a strong passphrase.
  2. Store the private key only on your own device(s).
  3. Keep an offline backup of the private key.
  4. Generate and store a revocation certificate.
  5. Share your public key through a verifiable channel.
  6. Rotate keys every few years or after any suspected compromise.
  7. Revoke and retire keys you no longer use.

The Three Operations

PGP can do three distinct things. Understanding the difference is essential:

  • Encrypt — Scrambles the message so only the recipient's private key can read it. Provides confidentiality: no one else can see the contents. But it does not prove who sent it. Anyone with the recipient's public key can encrypt.
  • Sign — Creates a cryptographic signature using your private key that anyone with your public key can verify. Provides authentication and integrity: the recipient knows it came from you and was not tampered with. But the message itself is not hidden — anyone can read it.
  • Sign and Encrypt — Does both. The message is signed with your private key, then encrypted with the recipient's public key. Provides confidentiality, authentication, and integrity all at once.

When to Use Each

Goal Use
Keep a message secret Encrypt only
Prove you wrote it (public statement) Sign only
Send a private, verifiable message Sign then Encrypt
Why "sign then encrypt"? If you encrypt first, then sign the ciphertext, an attacker could strip your signature and re-sign the ciphertext with their own key, making it look like they wrote the message. By signing the plaintext first and then encrypting the signed message, the signature is protected inside the encryption and cannot be tampered with.

Why Order Matters

The two possible orderings are:

  1. Sign then Encrypt (recommended): You sign the plaintext with your private key, then encrypt the (plaintext + signature) with the recipient's public key. The recipient decrypts first, then verifies your signature. This is the safest combination because the signature is bound to the original message and protected from tampering.
  2. Encrypt then Sign (not recommended): You encrypt the message, then sign the ciphertext. The signature is on the ciphertext, not the plaintext. An attacker can replace your signature with theirs, forwarding the same ciphertext and claiming they sent it. This is a real attack known as signature substitution.
DO: Use "sign then encrypt" for any message that needs both privacy and proof of origin. Most modern PGP tools do this by default when you select both options.
DON'T: Sign a ciphertext and assume that proves authorship. Always sign the plaintext before encrypting.

Why Key Size Matters

A larger key means more possible combinations an attacker must try to guess your private key. But bigger is not always better — larger keys are slower to generate, encrypt, and decrypt. The goal is to find the sweet spot: strong enough to resist attacks, fast enough to be practical.

RSA Key Sizes

RSA is the most widely supported public-key algorithm. It is based on the difficulty of factoring large numbers.

  • RSA-2048 — The practical minimum today. Fast to use, provides ~112 bits of security. Fine for low-stakes practice and learning.
  • RSA-3072 — A middle ground. Provides ~128 bits of security. Recommended for most real-world use if you want extra margin without much speed cost.
  • RSA-4096 — The gold standard for RSA. Provides ~150 bits of security. Slower to generate and use, but offers a strong safety margin. Recommended for sensitive communications.

ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography)

ECC is a newer algorithm that achieves the same security as RSA with much smaller keys. It is based on the mathematics of elliptic curves rather than factoring. Smaller keys mean faster operations and less storage — a big advantage on phones and embedded devices.

  • ECC-P256 — Provides ~128 bits of security (comparable to RSA-3072). Fast and widely supported. A good default choice.
  • ECC-P384 — Provides ~192 bits of security (comparable to RSA-7680). Used by the U.S. government for SECRET-level data.
  • ECC-P521 — Provides ~256 bits of security (comparable to RSA-15360). Overkill for most purposes, but offers the highest margin.

Security Equivalence Table

Security Level RSA Equivalent ECC Equivalent Use Case
112 bits RSA-2048 ECC-224 Practice / learning
128 bits RSA-3072 ECC-256 Standard use (recommended)
192 bits RSA-7680 ECC-384 Sensitive data, government
256 bits RSA-15360 ECC-521 Maximum security / long-term

How to Choose

DO: Use RSA-3072 or ECC-256 for everyday use. Use RSA-4096 or ECC-384 for sensitive communications like journalism, legal, or medical data. If you are unsure, RSA-4096 is always a safe choice.
DON'T: Use RSA-2048 for anything you care about long-term. It is acceptable for learning and practice (like this tool), but NIST recommends phasing it out by 2030. Also avoid ECC-521 unless you have a specific reason — it is slower than P384 with no practical security benefit.
Beginner tip: If you are just starting out, use RSA-4096. It is the most compatible and offers strong security. Once you are comfortable, consider switching to ECC-256 for speed. The security difference is negligible for practice purposes — what matters is that you understand the flow.

The "Store Now, Crack Later" Problem

An attacker can record your encrypted traffic today and store it for decades. If computers become fast enough to crack your key in the future, they can decrypt your old messages retroactively. This is why using strong keys matters even if you think your messages are not interesting today — they might be interesting to someone in 10 years.

What Is a Key Server?

A key server is a public directory where people can publish their PGP public keys. Think of it as a phone book for encryption keys — anyone can look up your key by your email address and use it to send you an encrypted message.

Popular Key Servers

  • keys.openpgp.org — The modern default. Clean, verified, and privacy-respecting. Requires email verification before publishing a key.
  • keyserver.ubuntu.com — Popular in the Linux community. Used for verifying software packages.
  • keys.mailvelope.com — Used by the Mailvelope browser extension for Gmail and Outlook.
  • pgp.mit.edu — One of the oldest keyservers. Still works but has spam and verification issues.

How Key Servers Work

The basic flow:

  1. You generate a key pair on your computer.
  2. You upload your public key to a key server.
  3. The key server verifies your email address (to prevent impersonation).
  4. Anyone can search for your email and download your public key.
  5. They verify the fingerprint matches what you told them (out-of-band verification).
  6. They encrypt a message to you using your public key.
Important: Never upload your private key to a key server. Key servers are for public keys only. Your private key should never leave your device.

The Verification Problem

Just because a key is on a key server does not mean it really belongs to the person it claims to. Anyone can upload a key claiming to be anyone. This is why fingerprint verification is critical — you must compare the key's fingerprint through a trusted channel (in person, over the phone, on a business card) before trusting it.

DO: Upload your public key to keys.openpgp.org after generating it. This makes it easy for people to find your key and send you encrypted messages.
DON'T: Trust a key from a server without verifying its fingerprint. A key server is a directory, not a certificate of identity. Use the Fingerprint Validator to practice this skill.

Key Revocation

If your private key is compromised (or you lose it), you need to tell the world to stop using your public key. You do this with a revocation certificate — a special signed message that says "this key is no longer trustworthy." You upload it to the key server, and anyone who refreshes their copy will see the key is revoked.

Generate a revocation certificate when you create the key and store it somewhere safe. If you wait until you need it, you may not have access to the key anymore.

The Web of Trust

The "Web of Trust" is a decentralized system where PGP users sign each other's keys to vouch for their identity. If Alice trusts Bob, and Bob has signed Charlie's key, Alice can have some confidence that Charlie's key really belongs to Charlie — even without meeting him.

In practice, the Web of Trust has largely been replaced by simpler verification methods (email verification, in-person fingerprint comparison) because it was too complex for most users. But the principle remains: trust is built through verified connections, not blind assumptions.

Passwords vs. Passphrases

A password is typically a short, complex string like Xq7$mP2!. It is hard to remember and easy to crack with automated tools. A passphrase is a sequence of random words like purple-river-bicycle-mango. It is longer, easier to remember, and much harder to crack.

Why Length Beats Complexity

Password strength is determined by entropy — the number of possible combinations an attacker must try. Each character you add doubles the search space. A 8-character password using all character types has about 52 bits of entropy. A 4-word passphrase using common English words has about 52 bits too — but a 6-word passphrase has 78 bits, and it is still easy to type and remember.

Modern GPUs can try billions of password guesses per second. An 8-character password can be cracked in hours. A 6-word passphrase would take centuries. Length is the single most important factor in password strength.

The math: There are about 7,000 common English words. A 4-word passphrase has 7,000^4 = 2.4 trillion combinations (~41 bits). A 6-word passphrase has 7,000^6 = 118 quintillion combinations (~67 bits). Adding one word multiplies the difficulty by 7,000.

How to Create a Good Passphrase

  1. Pick 4-6 random, unrelated words. Use a password strength checker to test.
  2. Do not use famous quotes, song lyrics, or common phrases — those are in every cracking dictionary.
  3. Do not use personal information (names, dates, pets) — those are easy to guess or look up.
  4. Add a number or symbol if the service requires it, but do not substitute letters (P@ssw0rd is in every wordlist).
  5. Use a different passphrase for every account. A password manager makes this practical.
DO: Use a passphrase like correct-horse-battery-staple for your password manager master password. Use the password manager to generate and store unique random passwords for every other account.
DON'T: Reuse passwords across accounts. If one service is breached, attackers will try the same password on every other service. This is the #1 way accounts get compromised.

Passphrases for PGP Keys

Your PGP private key should be protected by a passphrase. If someone steals your key file, the passphrase is the last line of defense. Without it, they cannot use the key.

Use a strong passphrase (6+ random words) for your PGP key. Since you only need to type it when decrypting or signing, a long passphrase is not a daily burden. The security payoff is enormous: even if your computer is stolen, your private key remains protected.

Password Managers

A password manager (like Bitwarden, KeePassXC, or 1Password) generates and stores unique, random passwords for every account. You only need to remember one strong master passphrase. The manager handles the rest — auto-filling passwords, syncing across devices, and alerting you to breaches.

Using a password manager is the single most impactful security habit you can adopt. It eliminates password reuse, generates strong passwords you could never remember, and saves time.

What Is OPSEC?

OPSEC (Operational Security) is the practice of protecting sensitive information by identifying, controlling, and eliminating indicators that an adversary could use. It is not a single tool or setting — it is a mindset. Every decision you make about how you communicate, store data, and present yourself online is an OPSEC decision.

The OPSEC Process

  1. Identify what you are protecting. Data, identity, access, location, relationships.
  2. Identify the threat. Who wants your data? What can they do? What resources do they have?
  3. Analyze vulnerabilities. Where are the weak points in your current setup?
  4. Assess risk. How likely is each threat? How bad is the consequence?
  5. Apply countermeasures. Tools and habits that reduce the risk to an acceptable level.

Metadata: What You Leak Without Knowing

Even when your message content is encrypted, metadata is often visible: who you talk to, when, how often, from where, and how large the messages are. Intelligence agencies have famously said "we kill people based on metadata." It reveals patterns and relationships that content encryption does not hide.

What metadata leaks from common tools:

  • Email (even PGP-encrypted): Sender, recipient, subject line, timestamp, IP addresses, message size.
  • SMS / phone calls: Both phone numbers, tower location, duration, timestamp.
  • Signal: Minimal — sealed sender hides even the sender's identity from the server.
  • Web browsing: Every site you visit, DNS queries, browser fingerprint, IP address.
Key principle: Encryption protects what you say. Anonymity tools protect that you said anything at all. For most people, encryption is sufficient. For activists, journalists, and sources, anonymity may also be necessary.

Digital Footprint & Data Minimization

Every account you create, every photo you post, every app you install leaves a trace. The principle of data minimization is simple: share as little as possible, delete what you no longer need, and assume anything you put online could become public.

  • Audit your social media accounts. Delete old posts that reveal personal information.
  • Use a separate email address for accounts you do not care about (shopping, newsletters).
  • Check Have I Been Pwned to see if your email appears in known breaches.
  • Review app permissions on your phone. Does that flashlight app really need your contacts?
  • When a service offers "sign in with Google/Facebook," consider whether you want them tracking that account.

Secure Communication Tools

Not all tools are created equal. Here is a quick comparison:

  • Signal — End-to-end encrypted messaging with minimal metadata. The gold standard for private chat. Free and open source.
  • PGP email — Encrypts content but leaks metadata (sender, recipient, subject). Good for long-form sensitive communication.
  • Tor Browser — Anonymizes your web browsing by routing through three volunteer-run servers. Hides your IP and location.
  • VPN — Encrypts your traffic between you and the VPN server. Hides traffic from your ISP and WiFi eavesdroppers. Does not provide anonymity (the VPN can see everything).
  • HTTPS — Encrypts traffic between you and a website. Essential but not sufficient — the website still sees your plaintext.
DO: Use Signal for sensitive conversations, Tor for anonymous browsing, PGP for sensitive email, a VPN on public WiFi, and a password manager everywhere. Layer your tools — no single tool solves everything.
DON'T: Rely on a VPN alone for anonymity. The VPN provider can see your traffic and knows your real IP. A VPN hides you from your ISP and local network, not from the VPN company. For true anonymity, use Tor.

Device Security

Your devices are where your keys live. If a device is compromised, your keys are compromised.

  • Enable full-disk encryption (FileVault on Mac, BitLocker on Windows, LUKS on Linux).
  • Keep your operating system and apps updated. Updates patch security vulnerabilities.
  • Use a screen lock (PIN, fingerprint, face unlock) with a reasonable timeout.
  • Do not install apps from unknown sources. On Android, avoid sideloading unless you trust the source.
  • On shared computers, assume everything you type can be captured (keyloggers). Never access sensitive accounts from a shared computer.

Social Engineering Awareness

The weakest link in any security system is the human. No amount of encryption helps if you willingly hand your password to someone who sounds trustworthy. Social engineering attacks exploit trust, urgency, authority, and fear to manipulate people into making bad decisions.

Practice spotting the red flags with the Social Engineering Red Flags Quiz. The golden rule: when in doubt, verify through a separate channel. If "IT" emails you, call them using the number from the company directory — not the one in the email.

OPSEC Is a Habit, Not a Checklist

Perfect security does not exist. The goal is not to be unhackable — it is to raise the cost of attack high enough that adversaries move on to easier targets. Every small improvement helps: enabling 2FA, using a password manager, switching to Signal, verifying a fingerprint.

Start with one thing today. Then add another next week. Over time, these habits become second nature, and your overall security posture improves dramatically. The Threat Modeling Practice Tool can help you figure out where to start.
